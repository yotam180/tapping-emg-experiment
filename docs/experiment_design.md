Research Proposal – Yotam Salmon
Introduction
Voluntary actions are thought to be shaped, at least in part, by their expected sensory outcomes. The ideomotor theory proposes that actions are represented in terms of their anticipated sensory effects (event files), and the selection of appropriate muscle activation patterns occurs through the activation of the relevant sensory effect representation. In the auditory domain, it has been shown that simple actions such as finger taps are modulated in force and timing by the predicted outcome specificity (knowing in advance which tone will be played), reliability (knowing whether it will be played at all), intensity (volume), and timing (delay in which it will be played).
It has also been shown that the reliability and specificity of expected auditory outcomes modulate the readiness potentials (RP, a slow buildup of activity in motor-related areas preceding voluntary movement) and post movement beta rebound (PMBR, post-movement increase in beta-band activity linked to movement termination and evaluation of sensory outcomes).
The proposed research asks whether these anticipated sensory consequences are also reflected in peripheral motor signals during action preparation and early execution. More specifically, we ask whether the specificity of identity or intensity of the anticipated stimulus is decodable from early surface electromyography (sEMG) beyond what is explained by action dynamics (such as force profile and timing) alone.
Methods
Experiment Setup
Participants will sit in front of a laptop and perform cue-guided, self-initiated keypresses with the index finger of their dominant hand against a flat, non-traveling pressure plate, while keeping the rest of their hand and arm as still as possible and rest against a table. The response surface will not produce neither a mechanical click nor any task-relevant tactile feedback to minimize sensory consequences unrelated to the experimental auditory outcome. 
Participants will perform the task in discrete trials. On each trial, a visual cue presented briefly (provisionally 300ms) will indicate the expected auditory outcome (predictable high, predictable low, or unknown). Participants will be instructed to wait between 1-2 seconds after the cue and then perform a single keypress, and trials with responses made too early or too late can be repeated or discarded. The cue presented for the next trial will follow a randomised inter-trial interval distributed uniformly between 2.5-4.5 seconds to reduce rhythmic responding and allow separation of cue-related, premovement, and movement-related signals.
Visual cues will consist of simple abstract symbols presented at the centre of the screen, chosen to avoid semantic associations with the predicted auditory outcome. The cues for PL, PH, and MIX conditions will be matched in overall visual complexity, size, and contrast so that condition differences will not be inherently driven by the low-level visual properties. To minimize cue confounds, cue to condition mapping will be counter balanced between participants. When no cue is present, a fixation display will remain on the centre of the screen.

Auditory feedback will be provided with a 20ms delay after each key press in three conditions indicated by the preceding visual cue:
1.	Predictable Low (PL): each press produces a low tone (steady sinewave in a 220Hz frequency for a 50-millisecond duration) or a low-intensity tone (volume should be adjusted based on earplugs we use).
2.	Predictable High (PH): each press produces a high tone (steady sinewave in a 988Hz frequency for a 50-millisecond duration) or a high-intensity tone (volume should be adjusted based on earplug capability or set to % increase from low-intensity tone).
3.	Mixed (MIX): each press produces either a low or high tone with equal probability, pseudo-randomized within the block.
The design is intended to manipulate the specificity and intensity of the predicted auditory outcome while keeping the action dynamics themselves as constant as possible. This follows prior work showing that both the overt action and the pre-movement neural activity are sensitive to how specifically that effect can be predicted.
The primary comparison will contrast trials in which the identity or intensity of the auditory outcome are known in advance with trials in which it is not. Specifically, the PL and PH conditions will together form the identity-specific (IS) condition, whereas the mixed condition will form an identity-unspecific (IU) condition. This comparison tests whether early peripheral motor signals differ as a function of outcome specificity.
A secondary, exploratory comparison will examine PL and PH trials directly in both identity and intensity dimensions. This analysis asks whether peripheral signals contain information not only about the specificity of the predicted outcome, but also about which specific outcome is predicted. Direct peripheral evidence for such identity- and intensity-specific differences is currently limited, and this analysis can be treated as exploratory rather than as the primary test of the study.
The experiment consists of two separate task variants, one manipulating the expected identity of the auditory outcome and one manipulating its expected intensity. Task order will be counter balanced across participants (half would start with the identity task and the other half would start with the intensity task). Each task will consist of 5 runs of 60 trials each, total of 300 trials per task. Trials from PL and PH conditions will be intermixed with MIX condition in all runs in order to reduce risk that decoding will rely on block-level confounds (fatigue, habituation…). Each run will consist of 15 PL and PH trials and 30 MIX trials (of them 15 will produce low auditory response and 15 high auditory response). Brief breaks will be required between runs, and a longer break between the tasks. 
Data Collection and Preprocessing
Two signal types will be recorded on each trial: the force trajectory generated during the press, and sEMG signal recorded from the acting forearm. Force will be measured from a flat force sensitive response surface and used both as behavioural readout of action dynamics and as a control variable in the later decoding analyses. sEMG will be recorded using X-trodes dry carbon electrode arrays connected to a 16-bit data acquisition unit (DAU). One possible setup is a 16-electrode 4 by 4 forearm array. The electrode placement remains to be determined as multiple reasonable options exist (e.g. placement over the forearm extensor compartments similar to prior work involving X-trodes electrode arrays, or placement over the forearm flexor compartment or other muscle groups directly engaged by the tapping movement). The sampling rate should also be confirmed before data collection as not enough evidence was found about the capabilities of the measurement device.

The force signal will be segmented into epochs aligned with press onset. Because the sensor output is not linearly proportional to force and may vary between sessions, the response surface will be calibrated using known weights so that force can be expressed in Newtons. From each trial, we will extract features such as peak force, rate of force development (slope), time to peak, press duration, and early impulse (area under the curve). These measures are motivated by prior work suggesting that the earliest portion of the force profile is less likely to reflect online correction and may be more informative about motor planning (Cao20).
The sEMG signals will be band-pass filtered to remove slow drift and non-physiological noise, and notch filtering at the local mains frequency will be applied if needed. Two signal representations will then be retained: the filtered raw signal (for time-resolved decoding analyses), and a smoothed, time-binned representation for simpler feature-based analysis. For instance, rectifying the signal and computing summary measures such as RMS or mean absolute value for each time bin. This is meant to allow lower-dimensional models with more interpretable features.
Analysis
The primary analyses will be conducted within-participant, as substantial inter-participant variability is expected in both force output and sEMG signal amplitudes. The analysis will compare three model classes: a force-only baseline, an sEMG-only model based on the coarse time-binned features, and a combined force + sEMG model. The key comparison is whether adding sEMG features improves condition decoding beyond what can be achieved from force features alone. Exploratory dimensionality reduction techniques such as PCA may also be considered if required to stabilize the trained models (Nai16).
Initial models will use simple linear classifiers such as logistic regression or linear SVM, with more complex approaches reserved for exploratory analyses if warranted. Evaluation will focus on out-of-sample decoding performance on held-out trials within participant (in a non-block-aware way). The strongest outcome would be above-chance decoding from early sEMG windows, together with evidence that sEMG only or combined sEMG + force models outperform a force-only baseline. The exact time windows need to be decided.
